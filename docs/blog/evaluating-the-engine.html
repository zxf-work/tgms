Post #7 · Evaluation

Six systems, one registry

Xiaofei Zhang · University of Memphis · July 2026 · report · code

We benchmarked our native storage engine against five systems — DuckDB, PostgreSQL 16, ClickHouse, Neo4j, and Memgraph — on one twelve-query bi-temporal registry, at 200k / 1M / 10M synthetic events plus a real messaging dataset. Before any timing counts, every (system, query) cell must return a canonical-hash-identical answer to our reference implementation: same rows, same order, same types. A faster number for an easier question is a mistake, not a result.

The finding nobody advertises: everything was expressible

Not one cell in the matrix is "unsupported." Bi-temporal semantics — corrections, belief states, half-open intervals, strict interval algebra — ported to SQL and to Cypher, with care. The gap between TGMS and general systems is not expressiveness. It is what the queries cost:

Correctness ported everywhere. Speed did not.

Specialists win their shape — and nobody else's

PostgreSQL owns indexed point lookups (0.3 ms, a warm B-tree is a hard floor). ClickHouse owns whole-window aggregation, and its lead grows with scale: 2× over native at 200k, 12× at 10M. And the graph engines lose their own home turf: on traversal, native wins by one to two orders of magnitude — reachability in 17 ms against 3.8–7.3 seconds, temporal motifs in 40 ms against 2.1–5.1 seconds. Cypher expressed the motif more elegantly than any SQL in the study, as a closed triangle pattern. It ran it 50–125× slower than our kernel. Query elegance and execution speed are independent axes.

Why do graph engines lose here? Every hop of a bi-temporal traversal re-filters by belief and validity on edge properties. No graph index in the study accelerates that; our engine's valid-time clustering exists for exactly those predicates.

The baselines audited us back

Honest baselines cut both ways. PostgreSQL's 0.5 ms lookups exposed that our point reads were rebuilding whole segments the index had already located — 76 ms fell to 1. ClickHouse answered a motif query our own cost model refused, exposing a guardrail false positive. Building the baselines fixed the engine: roughly a 10× end-to-end improvement on our worst operators came from defects that TGMS-only benchmarking could never see, because both our backends were slow in the same ways and agreement reads as health.

Storage, one accounting

All systems loaded from the same event log, whole store divided by the same row count: native 25.1 B/row (after generation collection), ClickHouse 78.4 (the only other compressed form), DuckDB 187.7, PostgreSQL 549.7 — of which two thirds is the covering indexes its fast lookups are made of. Every asymmetry is stated in the report; ratios without stated accounting are how our own earlier 0.22× storage claim went wrong, and we keep that correction in the record.

Everything regenerates from one command, and the raw records ship in the repo. The full report, capability matrix, and reproduction guide are in docs/eval.
