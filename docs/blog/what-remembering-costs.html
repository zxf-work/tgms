What remembering costs

Xiaofei Zhang · University of Memphis · August 2026 · records · code

A bi-temporal store promises to remember: what held in the world, what we believed, and what we corrected. Promises have prices. We measured this one along every axis the evaluation plan names — correction density, memory, cache state, threads, concurrent readers, recovery — on the same host and protocol as every published table.

storage, latency, memory — all at parity with a current-state store

Free until the first correction

We built a stripped configuration that keeps only currently-believed rows — and refuses past-belief queries rather than answering them wrongly — then swept correction density from 0 to 20% against the full store.

Latency tells the same story with a sharper edge: scan queries step 30–50% at the first corrections and grow roughly linearly after — and the multipliers grow with scale (at 10M, a mere 0.1% density already costs 4× on the interval join). Retention is not a flat tax; it is a usage-based bill, and at 20% density it even costs admissibility — one full-store query crosses the cost guardrail. If your workload never corrects, the two-clock design costs you nothing. That is the honest pricing table no bi-temporal system we know of publishes.

The memory bill, misdiagnosed and then repaid

The resource sweep found something worse than a cost: a cliff. At 10M events every process needed ~5.9 GB whatever the query — a 0.6 ms point lookup and a 4 s diff had identical peaks — and under a 4 GB cap the suite did not degrade, it died. We blamed our unbounded segment cache, published that attribution, and built the budget to fix it.

The budget's acceptance run refuted the diagnosis: with the cache capped, 2 GB still OOM'd, because the cache held only 794 MB. The dominant term was the statistics warm-up materializing all ten million rows — strings and a sha256 each — as one transient. Entry #10 in our running misdiagnosis table; the fix was a streaming fold.

The cold-start row is the honest one to sit with: a short-lived client gets its first answer from DuckDB 10–70× sooner. Our engine assumes a resident process; that is a real deployment constraint, now written down next to the numbers that assume it.

What transfers. Price your durability promise per axis, and let the acceptance run refute your diagnosis — ours did, twice this week.
